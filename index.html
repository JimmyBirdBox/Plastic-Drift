Sources & assumptions

This is a prototype. The structure is built for real datasets; some figures below are already real, others are placeholders shaped on published totals until the full files are imported.

River outfalls → sea

  • realNamed river figures on the globe (Klang 12,816 t/yr, Meycauayan 12,398, Lagos Harbour 3,999, Huangpu 3,650, Ebrie Lagoon 3,304, Sarawak 3,275, Rio Pavuna 2,553, Pearl River 2,485) come from the Meijer et al. (2021) model of 31,819 river outfalls.
  • approxCountry split of river emissions (Philippines largest, then India, Malaysia, China, Indonesia…) and a global total of about 1 Mt/yr follow Meijer et al. as summarised by Our World in Data. Remaining regions are an illustrative split.
  • approxEarlier years scale with global plastic production, from about 2 Mt in 1950.

Exported plastic scrap (grey)

  • approxExport volumes follow the shape of UN Comtrade HS 3915 totals (1988–2024): steady growth, a peak around 2012–2014, and a sharp drop after China's 2018 import ban. Country shares are approximate. Next step: load the full Our World in Data CSV.
  • modelRoutes go through real shipping lanes (Suez, Malacca, Luzon Strait) but transit time is stretched so shipments are visible.
  • modelShare of imported scrap assumed lost to sea: 3–5% depending on destination. This is an assumption, not a measurement.

In the sea

  • modelDrift uses a simplified model of the six ocean gyres (rotation direction is correct for each hemisphere). It is not a current simulation; next step is real surface-current data.
  • modelDrift speed ~3,000 km per year; sinking items settle after 2–18 months.
  • realDDT drums off Santa Catalina Island, California: industrial waste dumped in the mid-20th century. A 2021 survey mapped more than 27,000 barrel-like objects on the seabed.

Potential (2025–2040)

  • approxRiver emissions rise to about 2.6× the 2016 level by 2040, in line with the business-as-usual scenario in Breaking the Plastic Wave (Pew & SYSTEMIQ, 2020).
